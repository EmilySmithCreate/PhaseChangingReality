## The model in plain words

A black hole's mass is usually taken as fixed unless something falls in. In this model the mass is tied to the size of
the universe: as space stretches by a factor *a*, the mass grows as *a* to the power *k*. At *k* = 3 the growth exactly
cancels the thinning of the population by expansion, so once black holes are born their total energy density stays
constant, which is what dark energy does. The claim is that all of dark energy could be the sum of the universe's black
holes, born with the stars.

- **Farrah et al. 2023**, Astrophys. J. Lett. 944, L31 (arXiv:2302.07878). Elliptical galaxies' central black holes
  appear to have grown over seven billion years more than feeding could explain; read as *k* about 3. Disputed on the
  sample and the growth channels (to verify).
- **Croker et al. 2024** (arXiv number to verify) and earlier Croker and Weiner 2019. The mechanism, and
  a fit to supernovae and star-formation history.
- **Against it** (as reported in Ashley's look, to verify at source): gravitational-wave black-hole masses give *k*
  below about 2.1 at two sigma; a population growing as *a*³ would show in the mass distribution of merging pairs.

## What his look found (`sandbox/LOG.md`, 7 November; one home)

A flat background with no Lambda put in, black holes born with the star-formation history, fitted over the matter
share alone to 1,466 Pantheon+ supernovae: *k* = 3 fits as well as Lambda, *k* = 0 and 1 fail, and 95 % of the dark
energy is built by *z* = 1, so supernovae alone cannot tell *k* = 3 from Lambda. It needs 3.7 units of black-hole mass
per unit of stellar mass formed, a large number (to compare with the measured black-hole mass fraction, about one part
in a thousand of stellar mass; general knowledge, to verify).
